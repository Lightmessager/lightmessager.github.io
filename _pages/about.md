## About Me

I am Yixuan Fan, a fourth-year BSc Applied Chemistry student at Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University in Suzhou, China. My work sits at the intersection of computational chemistry and homogeneous catalysis: I use density functional theory, primarily Gaussian, to map reaction landscapes for organometallic systems and to understand how catalysts choose one pathway over another. Alongside mechanism work, I am equally drawn to the tools themselves — optimising workflows, automating input generation, and thinking about how computational chemistry software could be made more efficient and more transparent.

I entered this field when Dr. Xiaotai Wang invited me into his group, and I have stayed with it because the problems keep getting more interesting. What hooked me was the particular way a mechanism forces you to think: identify a plausible site, propose an intermediate, test it against the energy surface, and revise when the numbers disagree. That loop — hypothesis, calculation, diagnosis, revision — is still what I enjoy most.

**What I can do independently.** Given an organometallic substrate, I can take a project from a clean structure to a complete catalytic cycle. That includes building the molecular model, locating transition states and intermediates, working through conformational alternatives or proposing non-literature pathways, and assembling the full energetic cycle from single-point energies — including in mixed-solvent environments, where the extra degrees of freedom change the problem substantially. Organic systems are generally more straightforward; the organometallic work is where I have spent most of my effort.

**What I treat as evidence of capability.** Across two SURF projects and my ongoing work with Dr. Wang, three things stand out. First, when a transition state refused to converge after roughly thirty rounds of bond-length and conformational adjustment, I did not treat it as a failed calculation — I traced it to an under-coordinated nickel centre and proposed carbonyl-assisted reductive elimination as a chemically reasoned alternative. That diagnosis is, to me, the more useful result. Second, I have carried a full computational workflow from geometry optimisation to final energy cycle without supervision. Third, while working through a published JACS study on Co/Ni cross-coupling, I identified a follow-up direction that the literature had not addressed; it remains on my list, deferred only by time rather than by lack of interest. I also built the SURF poster myself — text, typesetting, and the communication with the supervising professor — though the poster itself was created under time pressure and I would not call its layout a fair reflection of the work.

**My academic base.** My strongest formal preparation is in Organic and Inorganic Chemistry, with particular weight on coordination chemistry, group theory, and reaction mechanisms; my marks in those areas are 89, 82, and 75 respectively. I am less practised in laboratory organic synthesis, and I treat that as a deliberate division of effort rather than a gap to apologise for. My default learning mode is self-directed: take the underlying skills and knowledge, work through them until they are internal, then extend outward by asking questions and testing the theory against edge cases. I picked up Python the same way, and I am now learning C/C++ with the same aim — to understand what is happening underneath the calculation rather than simply running it.

**Outside the calculations.** I serve as President of the Chemistry Society at XJTLU, after a term as Head of Planning. I inherited a society that was close to dormant, with no outgoing president to hand over from, and rebuilt it from a low base in a department where chemistry is not a popular major. The structure I introduced — broad entry, selective retention — let every member try a provisional leadership role and kept the ones who proved they wanted it. I also redesign our public-facing experiments around safety as a non-negotiable constraint, selecting raw materials carefully and teaching laboratory-safety basics alongside each activity. We ran sessions on perfume extraction, soap and hand-cream making, and weather bottles; the participation was strong, and more importantly it shifted how students in other majors talked about chemistry. I have given five lectures in this capacity, three exam-oriented reviews and two public-interest sessions, and contributed as a guest lecturer on crystal-structure extensions and organic-reaction mechanisms.

I keep the society work separate from my research, but it shapes how I work: I am comfortable taking something that is not working, figuring out which constraint actually matters, and rebuilding around it.

**What comes next.** I am applying for direct-entry PhD positions in computational groups, with two broad aims. One is to help design new catalysts or use computation to predict and develop new materials; the other is to work on the computational tools themselves — making them faster, more visual, and easier to use correctly. A group that bridges those two, or that lets me move between them, would be an ideal fit. My current interests are organometallic catalytic mechanisms, computational materials discovery, and the optimisation and development of computational-chemistry software. I am less drawn to synthetic laboratory work as a primary focus.

When I am not at a terminal, I write fiction, mostly about people rather than about chemistry, and I play video games — *Wuthering Waves*, *Minecraft*, *Slay the Spire*. I am reading *La Porte étroite* and working through an animated C-language course on Bilibili, because I find I learn technical material much better when there is a figure moving on the screen.

I am open to discussion on transition-state location, Gaussian workflows, and organometallic mechanisms, and I am happy to share the small tools I have written for batch-processing log files.
